A few weeks ago, President Ochtdorf took me aside and said, "Patrick, your talks, they're
okay, but where's the aviation?"
So here's just a little aviation.
I spent much of my childhood at boarding school in England, looking up at aeroplanes crossing
the sky dreaming of flying home to my parents thousands of miles away in Arabia, I yearned
to fly. I wanted to become a fighter pilot. The Royal Air Force was recruiting pilots
and the fastest way for me to fly was to learn to glide. A glider is a lightweight aircraft
with long slender wings and no engine that stays aloft by riding within, rising currents
of air.
So in my mid-teens, I spent part of two summers at Nairfield in Oxfordshire, learning to fly
a glider with the air force.
First, I learned to fly straight and level, then make stable turns, to take off, towed
by a tugplane and finally to land safely back at the airfield. Only then was I ready to learn
to stay in the air, climb and eventually soar all without an engine.
The crucial skill in gliding is finding rising air. You cannot see it, but you can see what it does.
I was taught to look for cumulus clouds because they often mark where warm air is rising
and I learned to fly toward them.
But even more important than using visual cues
to find rising air is to learn to feel it.
While flying along, I would begin to feel
that I was being lifted by an unseen force.
The rising air is called lift.
One wing would tip upward and I would turn toward it
into the lift.
I was taught how to stay in lift, circling upward, gaining altitude,
and often join other pilots who had found lift,
climbing within the same invisible rising column of air.
To be flying is fantastic,
but climbing in lift and soaring with no engine is almost beyond description.
For me, it was beautiful.
with only the whistle of the wind, the occasional creak from the glider,
and the magnificent sensation of being drawn upward.
Just as there is rising air, there is also sinking air,
helpfully called sink.
It likewise cannot be seen, but it can be felt, and I quickly learn to avoid it.
Gliding into sink can feel like falling sometimes quickly.
it shortens the distance you can fly and may threaten the safe landing.
When I encountered sync, I learned to take to turn away decisively.
A decade after those gliding days and not far into my journey of faith,
I recognized that principles of gliding I had learned in my teens
have significant similarities with spiritual principles.
We must avoid sync and find lift.
At the heart.
