## The concept

Six teams work through six missions to turn conference teaching into help for an ordinary week. Each mission combines a short conference excerpt, a small card challenge, and a situation youth could encounter. Every team plays every mission at the same time. Teams earn points for finding evidence and explaining a practical response.

The game question is **“What does this teaching help us do?”** Speaker names are visible as attribution. The six fireside assignments provide the structure; broader conference themes can appear in optional challenges. The full group questions remain in [Conference Discussion Notes.md](Conference%20Discussion%20Notes.md).

Keep the playful elements in the team names, simultaneous reveals, sorting cards, mystery objects, and challenge clock. Treat teachings and personal struggles respectfully. Use fictional situations; participants can contribute an example or write without sharing personal experiences.

## Teams and room setup

Use the six existing assigned groups as teams when their sizes are reasonably balanced. That honors the listening assignments and gives each group expertise to bring. With 30 youth, teams average five; with 50, they average eight or nine. If one group is much larger, balance numbers before play and retain its assigned topic for the expert exchange. Do not put 25 youth on each of two teams.

At each table, start discussion in pairs or a trio so quieter participants have space. Rotate the writer, card arranger, spokesperson, and evidence checker each mission. Make sure everyone has a turn in some role; speaking to the whole room is optional.

Use one laptop, one shared screen, and adequate sound. Teams need a numbered response booklet, topic cards, challenge cards, pencils, and a six-space progress strip. One host manages the screen and clock; two circulating adults are helpful for six tables. No participant phone or account is needed.

All teams remain at their tables. The task changes on the screen. This avoids six competing audio sources and the equipment/staff requirements of video stations.

## A mission takes five minutes

1. **Notice — 45 seconds:** show the two evidence questions and play a 20–50-second excerpt. Add a one-sentence introduction if its context is essential. Allow a replay when needed.
2. **Solve — 60 seconds:** pairs discuss, then the team locks its two evidence answers by writing or arranging cards. Everyone reveals together.
3. **Connect and apply — 105 seconds:** name a fireside topic and the clue supporting it, then solve the scenario with a two-sentence response or the specified card task.
4. **Reveal — 90 seconds:** show the evidence answers, acceptable connections, and application checklist. One or two teams give a brief example; rotate those teams over the activity. Teams check their written answers and record points while adults circulate.

The six-space strip records completed missions, even if a team misses an evidence question. It tracks progress rather than access to the rest of the game. Each team leaves with a page of ideas it helped create.

## Scoring that a host can manage

Each mission offers **five points**, for **30 total**:

| Part | Points | What earns them |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Evidence | 2 | One for each correct listening answer, specified below. |
| Connection | 1 | Name one fireside topic and explain it using an actual clue from the excerpt. More than one topic may fit. |
| Application: teaching | 1 | Apply a relevant teaching to the fictional situation. Naming a religious word without explaining the connection is insufficient. |
| Application: action | 1 | Give a concrete, feasible next step that fits the situation and respects the people involved. |

Write answers before the reveal. Teams use the displayed key to check their own work; circulating adults help with ambiguous applications and spot-check responses. Do not require the host to judge six speeches every round. Accept thoughtful alternatives, including those different from the sample responses below. Points measure work on the challenge, not the sincerity of anyone's faith or the strength of a testimony.

Do not use speed bonuses, stealing, elimination, or wagers. For a tie, share the win. Recognize useful teamwork and ideas in the closing without adding subjective points. A low-stakes team reward can go with the game score; keep all youth included in refreshments.

## Default running order: 50 minutes

| Time | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Form tables, explain the five-point rubric, and demonstrate a response. |
| 5–35 | Six five-minute missions, one anchored in each assignment. |
| 35–41 | Expert exchange: each original group offers one useful insight from its listening assignment. |
| 41–49 | Final challenge: combine three topics to help a fictional friend. |
| 49–50 | Announce score, recognize teamwork, and invite a private next step. |

For **40 minutes**, use four-minute missions, a four-minute expert exchange, and a seven-minute finale after a four-minute setup, with a one-minute close. Keep answers short and play one excerpt per mission. For **60 minutes**, add the two four-minute optional challenges and two minutes of breathing room. If a separate fireside discussion must fit within the event, shorten this game rather than silently consuming its time.

## Six missions and host keys

Times below are **candidate windows in original videos**, not final edits or prepared clips. Use the captions and then check the audio to establish clean cuts. The existing clip files do not include every proposed passage. Read a clearly labeled paraphrase if a new clip is not ready. The old speaker-identification app does not yet run these missions.

### 1. Promise or overpromise?

**Anchor:** Deacons — Following Jesus Christ. **Source:** Patricio M. Giuffra, approximately **04:01–04:32**. Introduce: he is explaining why we keep commandments and what obedience does and does not promise.

Give each team four statement cards: *Love for God*, *Guaranteed easy life*, *Closeness to the Savior*, and *No future difficulties*. After listening, sort them into **Taught here** and **Overpromise**.

**Two evidence points:** one for placing *Love for God* in Taught here, and one for placing both easy-life/no-difficulties cards in Overpromise. *Closeness to the Savior* also belongs in Taught here; use it for the connection discussion rather than a third evidence point.

**Scenario:** “You've been trying to follow Jesus, but you still had a rough week. A friend says, ‘Then what's the point?’ Write a reply that names a worthwhile result and something you can keep doing.”

**Host key:** love for God motivates obedience; closeness and protection from sin do not guarantee no trials. Accept Deacons or Hope with a supported explanation. An application might explain that following Christ can change how we respond, then choose one act of honesty, patience, prayer, or kindness tomorrow. Avoid promising that obedience will make the friend popular or solve the week immediately.

### 2. Replace the label

**Anchor:** Builders of Faith — Identity and worth. **Source:** Hutch U. Fale, approximately **05:32–06:13**. Introduce: he is explaining how understanding God's love affects the labels we put on ourselves and others.

Give teams label cards such as *Failure*, *Weird*, *Not good enough*, and *Someone worth knowing*. They choose one harsh label to replace with a statement reflecting divine worth, then describe how treatment would change. The labels refer to fictional characters, not people in the room.

**Two evidence points:** Does Fale apply God's love to **ourselves only** or **ourselves and others**? Does he encourage being **quick to judge** or **slow to judge**? **Answers:** ourselves and others; slow to judge.

**Scenario:** “After a mistake, Alex says, ‘I'm just a failure.’ Meanwhile, another classmate is being left out because others think they're weird. Choose one person, replace the label, and name one helpful action.”

**Host key:** a mistake or social label does not define a child's divine worth. Accept Builders, Gatherers, or Deacons with evidence. Actions could include helping Alex repair the mistake, inviting the excluded classmate to sit together, or challenging an unkind label without humiliating anyone. A vague compliment alone needs a next step for the action point.

### 3. Find the signal; take a step

**Anchor:** Teachers — The Holy Ghost and personal revelation. **Source:** Patrick Kearon, approximately **04:54–05:20**. Introduce his gliding image: rising air cannot be seen, but a pilot learns to notice its effect and respond.

Teams arrange three process cards: **Seek**, **Recognize**, **Act**. Add a clearly labeled supporting paraphrase from Soares (**02:18–03:15**): intentional prayer joined with fasting and humility can help prepare us to receive revelation. The three-stage arrangement summarizes the assigned question; do not attribute all three card labels as an exact quotation from Kearon.

**Two evidence points:** Does Kearon describe lift as necessarily **visible** or something **unseen that can be felt**? Does he ask us only to **notice** impressions or to **discern and act**? **Answers:** unseen that can be felt; discern and act.

**Scenario:** “You notice someone sitting alone and have a quiet thought to include them. You aren't sure whether it was a prompting. Build a simple plan for seeking guidance and taking a kind next step.”

**Host key:** a small, constructive action can be a response while continuing to seek guidance. Accept Teachers, Gatherers, or Deacons with a clue. A possible plan is to pray for help, invite the person to join, and keep listening. If discussing a larger decision, consulting a trusted adult can be a useful application. Do not make every emotion a guaranteed revelation or demand certainty before a kindness.

### 4. Repair the bad advice

**Anchor:** Messengers of Hope — Strength through challenges. **Source:** Tamara W. Runia, approximately **02:12–02:36**. Introduce: she has described thinking faithful living would keep every storm away. Optional second source: Hathaway **02:12–02:43**, or read a supporting paraphrase that painful questions and emotions are not sin or weakness.

Show two object cards: an **umbrella** and a **rock**. Teams choose the object that matches Runia's final explanation. Then they cross out the harmful part of this fictional advice: “If you had enough faith, this wouldn't hurt. Just stop worrying, and it will all be fixed tomorrow.” Rewrite it in two helpful sentences.

**Two evidence points:** Which object represents the gospel's support during a storm? Does Runia say faithful people **avoid every storm** or **still experience storms**? **Answers:** rock; still experience storms.

**Scenario:** “Your friend is struggling even though they've prayed and tried to do what is right. Write something that acknowledges the difficulty and offers a next step.”

**Host key:** Christ can sustain someone while the problem remains. Accept Hope, Deacons, or Priests with an explained connection. Examples can include listening, praying together if wanted, involving a trusted adult, or helping with a practical burden. A response can include questions or uncertainty. The action point requires more than telling the person to be positive.

### 5. From Sunday to Monday

**Anchor:** Priests — Covenants, sacrament, and temple preparation. **Source:** Christian C. Chigbundu, approximately **05:58–06:42**. Supporting paraphrase: Farnes **03:04–04:09** connects taking Christ's name with reflecting His qualities and ministering during the week.

Give teams three promise cards: **Take Christ's name**, **Remember Him**, **Keep His commandments**. Match them with possible Monday actions. An action can fit more than one promise when the team explains why; there is no exclusive matching key.

**Two evidence points:** one for identifying all three promises in the excerpt; one for identifying the relationship the sacrament strengthens — **our relationship with the Savior**.

**Scenario:** “On Monday, a group chat starts making fun of someone. What would remembering Jesus change about your response? Add one thing a youth can do now to prepare for temple worship.”

**Host key:** connect a promise with Christlike behavior. A feasible action could be refusing to join the mockery, speaking kindly, or checking on the person. Preparation could include learning about covenants, working on family history, or planning appropriate temple participation. The temple examples are applications supported by Gong/Chigbundu, not claims that every example appears in this short clip. Accept Priests, Deacons, or Gatherers with a clue. Do not test personal worthiness or discuss private temple details.

### 6. The interruption mission

**Anchor:** Gatherers of Light — Loving, serving, inviting. **Source:** Timothy L. Farnes, approximately **04:26–04:50**. Introduce: he is describing the Good Samaritan's response to a wounded traveler.

Teams put three cards in order: **See the person → Feel compassion → Act**. Then choose between two response cards: **Keep moving because they interrupted your plans** and **Look for a useful way to help**.

**Two evidence points:** one for the correct sequence; one for identifying the encounter as an **opportunity to minister**, rather than only an inconvenience. Explain that Farnes applies this pattern to ordinary encounters; youth are not being asked to handle emergencies on their own.

**Scenario:** “You invited a friend to a youth activity. They declined, and now they seem uncomfortable around you. Plan a next move that helps them feel loved and leaves them free to choose.”

**Host key:** continue friendship and interest without making attendance a condition. Rasband **00:58–01:43** supplies the agency/pressure context; offer that supporting paraphrase before the scenario. A possible response is to ask about the friend's week, include them in an ordinary shared activity, and leave the invitation open without repeated pressure. Accept Gatherers, Builders, Priests, or Deacons when the clue supports the connection.

## Expert exchange: keep the original assignments meaningful

Each assigned group has one minute to offer an insight from its topic: **a teaching, why it matters, and one possible action**. They may use the reviewed notes if they did not record a passage themselves. If game teams had to be balanced, briefly let original groups huddle or nominate a representative beforehand.

No extra points depend on this segment. Other groups jot down an idea they might borrow for the finale. Participants may pass or let someone read their written contribution. This rewards the original preparation with a real role while giving everyone access to all six themes.

## Final challenge: “Help Sam this week”

**Fictional situation:** “Sam has been left out, made a mistake, and feels discouraged. Sam says, ‘Maybe I'm not worth much, and I don't know what to do next.’ Your team has the six topic cards and the teachings you've collected.”

Choose **three topics**. Build a plan with **one helpful sentence**, **one specific action within 24 hours**, and **one source of continued support**. Underline which conference teaching supports each part. This permits different combinations: identity + revelation + hope, or identity + covenants + service, for example.

Give teams three minutes to prepare, two minutes to exchange plans with a neighboring team, and three minutes for two short examples and a host reflection. It is a cooperative finish with no additional score. Invite each youth to choose a private next step if they wish; do not collect or rank personal commitments.

**Sample plan:** remind Sam that one mistake does not erase divine worth (Fale); sit with Sam tomorrow and help repair the mistake (Fale/Farnes); keep seeking guidance and support through prayer and a trusted adult while the discouragement is still present (Kearon/Hathaway/Eyring). This is one valid plan, not the required answer.

## Optional conference challenges

Use at most two in a 60-minute version. They broaden the conference conversation while six of eight challenges remain anchored in the assignments. They can also connect back to those assignments.

**“Where will this lead?” — D. Todd Christofferson, approximately 06:44–07:29.** Introduce the actual subject: gambling and its opportunity costs. Give a fictional teenager a schedule and an invitation to place an online bet. Teams identify what time/resources could be displaced, then choose a constructive alternative. Evidence answers: present choices affect the future; doing one thing costs the opportunity to do another. Connection: discipleship or care for others. Score with the same five-point rubric. Use ordinary time cards, with no stakes or simulated betting.

**“A message you can use” — Vaiangina Sikahema, approximately 05:00–05:33.** Introduce: his grandparents made a difficult voyage to hear an apostle; modern access can be taken for granted. Teams receive a mock phone notification that a conference talk is available. Identify why access alone is insufficient, then turn one teaching from the missions into a concrete weekly reminder. Evidence answers: people still seek prophetic guidance; easy device access can be taken for granted. Connection: discipleship, with a distinction between prophetic teaching and personal revelation. Score with the same rubric.

## Rationale for the mission alternative

The six assignments are useful lenses that often overlap. A strict one-correct-topic quiz would penalize real connections, and a speaker quiz mainly measures recognition. This format gives clear listening answers while allowing multiple defensible applications. Simultaneous tabletop tasks let 30–50 youth work throughout, and the varied card challenges give the host something more active than six repeated discussion questions.

A station rotation would work if enough rooms, leaders, and screens were available, but that infrastructure is unconfirmed. A single-screen team game can use the existing assets and ordinary printed materials. It also continues smoothly if a video fails: read its prepared paraphrase and keep the mission moving.
